About

Reading the Galaxy's history in its stars today.

I am a postdoctoral researcher at the University of Oklahoma, working under Prof. Michael Hayden. My interest is anything that has to do with a living star — a thread that has carried through every stage of my career so far.

After a Bachelor's degree in India, I began my Master's at KU Leuven, where I worked on understanding the formation of massive stars, specifically Be stars, through binary modelling and spectroscopic analysis. At the Indian Institute of Astrophysics, I applied similar methods to low-mass stars, this time to understand the formation and evolution of blue straggler stars found within globular clusters.

My PhD extended this into exploring the chemical signatures and kinematics of current-day stellar populations (mostly low-mass), to understand the formation and evolution history of the Milky Way. In my postdoc, I'm continuing this pursuit of understanding our Galaxy by developing and using machine learning algorithms to analyse large-scale spectroscopic surveys such as 4MOST, WEAVE, GALAH, APOGEE, and DESI.

I'm also committed to mentoring: I've spent the past several years supervising high-school and undergraduate students on short-term astrophysics projects, and I look forward to staying closely involved with graduate student research as my own group grows.

Halo · thin & thick disk · bulge

Galactic archaeology

I look at chemical signatures from large-scale spectroscopic surveys and stellar kinematics from Gaia to study the different components of the Milky Way — the halo, the thin and thick disk, and the bulge. The goal is to extrapolate into the past from what we can measure today, to answer how our Galaxy came to be.

Globular & open clusters

Multiple stellar populations within stellar clusters

Globular clusters have turned out to be far more complicated than once thought — showing scatter in metallicity and anti-correlations between light elements like C, N, O, and Na that stellar evolution alone can't explain. Where this comes from is still an open question, and I'm using 4MOST and WEAVE to dig into it.

Li-rich giants · Ba stars · Be stars · blue stragglers

Formation & evolution of exotic stars

By exotic stars I mean things like lithium-rich giants, barium-rich stars, Be stars, and blue stragglers — objects with several possible formation pathways whose relative contributions aren't well understood. I use stellar evolution codes like MESA, binary modelling tools like PHOEBE, and spectroscopic analysis to work out which pathway produced which star.

LRPayne

Machine learning for spectroscopy

Recent surveys have already observed millions of stars, and WEAVE and 4MOST alone will add close to 20 million more over the next five years — far more than traditional analysis methods can keep up with. I build machine learning tools like LRPayne to speed up that analysis, and in some cases to recover abundances traditional methods can't, getting more science out of the same data.
